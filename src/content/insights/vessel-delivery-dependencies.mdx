From the outside, delivering cargo to a vessel looks like a transportation problem: collect it, move it, hand it over. In practice, it's a chain of dependencies, and any one of them slipping can put the whole delivery at risk.

## The chain

1. **Supplier readiness** — is the cargo actually ready to collect when planned?
2. **Documentation** — is the paperwork accurate and complete before it's needed?
3. **Customs** — has the applicable clearance process been started early enough?
4. **Storage** — does the cargo need to be held, and for how long, before the vessel is ready?
5. **Transportation** — is the movement scheduled around the vessel's actual delivery window, not a generic one?
6. **The vessel window itself** — ETAs change. The plan needs room to absorb that.

## Why coordination matters more than any single step

Each step in that chain can be handled competently in isolation and the delivery can still fail — because nobody was responsible for how they fit together. That's the gap port execution exists to close: one party tracking the whole chain against the vessel's actual schedule, not just their own piece of it.
